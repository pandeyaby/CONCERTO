\begin{abstract}
Production services run several autonomous reliability agents at once: an
autoscaler, load shedders, concurrency and rate limiters, retry budgets, and
outlier ejection. Each is designed, documented and validated alone, yet all
act on one plant. We study how such agents fail \emph{together}, and how to
test for it. We distinguish three coupling channels. Actuation coupling sums
gains past a delay margin no single agent can observe. Observation coupling
lets one agent hide demand from another. Topological coupling redistributes
load past a branching threshold. A model shows that observation coupling
yields a \emph{threshold-ordering trap}: when a shedder engages below the
autoscaler's target, the fleet settles under-provisioned. An audit of
Kubernetes, Envoy, Istio and Linkerd finds no configuration surface that
coordinates provisioning with protection. In pre-registered tests on
unmodified Kubernetes and Envoy, the trap reproduced in all 9 below-target
trials across three shedder types and two application shapes (attainment
0.29--0.50, 2--3 of 5 replicas). All 8 above-target trials escaped (attainment
$\ge$0.99). A threshold-aware shared authority budget (P1), started from
the trap configuration, passed its pre-registered test ($n=5$ per arm):
attainment 0.994 vs.\ 0.269 for independent agents and 1.000 for a
no-shedding reference, with every trial reaching 6 replicas. Its
pre-registered latency claim failed, an earlier run was inconclusive, and
other remedies were negative or marginal; we report all of it. We release the simulator,
testbed, pre-registrations and raw traces.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Ask an operator how their service protects itself under load and you will
get a list. There is an autoscaler that adds replicas when utilization
rises. There is a load shedder that rejects low-priority work when latency
climbs. There is outlier detection that ejects unhealthy endpoints. There is
a concurrency limiter that caps in-flight requests, a rate limiter that caps
outbound calls, a timeout policy, a retry budget, and a router that moves
traffic away from trouble.

Each of these is a feedback controller. Each was designed in isolation,
documented in isolation, and tuned in isolation. Most are configured through
different surfaces, and in a large organization most are owned by different
teams. And every one of them observes some function of the same underlying
congestion state and acts, directly or indirectly, on that same state.

Control engineers have a name for this situation. When several actuators
have authority over one plant, the assignment of corrective effort among
them is the control allocation problem, and it has been treated rigorously
for decades in aerospace, where an aircraft has more control surfaces than
axes of motion \cite{durham1993,johansen2013}. Service fleets have the same
structure and do not use the same discipline. We allocate nothing. We run
every controller at full authority and hope.

This paper argues that the hope is misplaced in a specific and predictable
way, and that the resulting failure modes are invisible from inside any
individual controller.

\subsection{Reliability controllers as autonomous agents}

Each of these mechanisms is, in the sense used by the agent literature, an
autonomous agent: it perceives part of the environment through its own
signal, decides on its own policy, and acts without a human in the loop or a
protocol with its peers. A fleet is therefore an uncoordinated multi-agent
system whose agents were each validated alone. We treat the problem as one
of \emph{verifying and testing agent interaction}: which properties of the
composition can be checked statically from configuration, which require
execution, and what an arbiter that mediates between agents buys. We do not
claim these controllers learn or plan; the framing concerns autonomy and the
absence of coordination, which is exactly what makes interaction failures
hard to test for one agent at a time.

\subsection{A failure no controller can see}

Consider a fleet with an autoscaler and a load shedder, both configured with
defaults that a careful operator would consider reasonable. Load rises. The
shedder sees latency climb and begins rejecting requests, which is exactly
its job, and latency recovers. The autoscaler, meanwhile, reads request rate
at the service, sees it fall, concludes that demand has dropped, and scales
the fleet down. Note that this requires an autoscaler configured on a
request-rate (custom or external) metric; the Kubernetes default is CPU
utilization \cite{k8shpa}, under which shed requests that never reach the pods
have the same effect only if they would have consumed CPU. In our simulation
(Section \ref{sec:results}) the outcome is not the growing oscillation often
described but a stable trap: when the shedder trips below the autoscaler's
target, the fleet ratchets down and sheds persistently.

Every controller in this story behaved exactly as specified. The shedder shed
under latency pressure. The autoscaler scaled to observed demand. Neither
configuration is wrong. The fault is in the composition, and neither
controller's telemetry contains enough information to attribute it, because
each one sees only its own loop closing correctly.

We are explicit about scope. Our central evidence is the audit and pre-registered testbed experiments on
the observation-coupling trap. Arbitration is discussed briefly (Section \ref{sec:design}). One
principle, P1, has a positive pre-registered result on one testbed; the rest
of the evidence is negative, inconclusive or marginal. No claim here concerns
production systems.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The mechanisms}

We use the following terms for the controllers a modern fleet runs. An
\emph{autoscaler} adjusts replica count from a utilization or demand signal.
A \emph{load shedder} or admission controller rejects a fraction of offered
work, usually prioritized, when a congestion signal exceeds a threshold. A
\emph{circuit breaker} or outlier detector removes an endpoint from the
serving set when its error rate or latency exceeds a threshold. A
\emph{concurrency limiter} caps in-flight requests. A \emph{rate limiter}
caps request rate. A \emph{timeout policy} bounds per-call latency. A
\emph{retry policy} reissues failed calls, typically bounded by a
\emph{retry budget}. A \emph{router} distributes traffic across endpoints and
performs failover.

\section{Three Coupling Channels}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

We distinguish coupling channels by what the controllers share.

\subsection{Actuation coupling}

Two controllers exhibit actuation coupling when their outputs move the same
plant variable in the same direction.

A concurrency limiter and a rate limiter both bound offered load. A shedder
and a rate limiter both bound admitted work. An autoscaler and an outlier
detector both change effective serving capacity, in opposite directions. In
each case the plant receives the sum of the corrections while each
controller was tuned as though it were applying the whole correction alone.

Within a single proxy, Envoy already recognizes one such interaction: its
retry budget is configured as a circuit-breaker threshold that bounds
concurrent retries to a fraction of active requests \cite{envoycb}.

\subsection{Observation coupling}

Two controllers exhibit observation coupling when one controller's actuation
lies upstream of the other's measurement point, so that the second
controller's input is a function of the first controller's output.

The autoscaler and shedder interaction of Section I is the canonical case.
Shedding removes work before it reaches the point where request rate is
measured, so the provisioning controller reads a demand signal that the
protection controller is actively suppressing. The same structure appears
whenever a protective mechanism is deployed at the edge of a measurement
boundary, which is the normal place to deploy one.

We expect observation coupling to be the least visible of the three. Actuation coupling at least produces a plant that responds
too strongly, which an operator may recognize as overshoot. Observation
coupling produces a controller that is behaving correctly on false data.

\subsection{Topological coupling}

Two controllers exhibit topological coupling when one controller's action
redistributes load and thereby changes the state the other controller
observes at a different endpoint.

Ejection and failover routing are both redistributive. Removing an endpoint
does not remove its traffic; it moves it. If survivors were already near
saturation, the redistribution pushes them past the threshold that triggered
the first ejection, and the process compounds. Retry amplification is a
temporal variant: a timeout reduction converts slow successes into failures,
failures into retries, and retries into additional offered load, so a
tightening action increases the very quantity it was meant to reduce.

\section{A Model of Overlapping Authority}
\label{sec:model}

We model the fleet around an operating point and treat each coupling channel
in turn. The models are deliberately minimal. Their purpose is to identify
the structural condition under which composition fails, not to predict
trajectories.

\subsection{Setup}

Let $x(t)$ denote a scalar congestion state at an endpoint, for instance
queue depth or latency deviation from setpoint, normalized so that $x=0$ is
the operating point. Let controllers $i = 1, \ldots, m$ observe $x$ and
apply corrections $u_i$. Each controller has an actuation delay $\tau_i > 0$
comprising its measurement window, its decision interval, and the physical
time for its action to take effect. For an autoscaler $\tau$ includes
container start time and is often tens of seconds; for a shedder it is
milliseconds.

\subsection{Actuation coupling breaks the delay margin}

Consider $m$ controllers with overlapping authority, each applying
proportional feedback $u_i = -g_i x(t - \tau)$ with common delay $\tau$ and
gain $g_i > 0$. Acting alone, controller $i$ closes the loop
\[
\dot{x}(t) = -g_i\, x(t - \tau) + d(t) ,
\]
which is asymptotically stable if and only if $g_i \tau < \pi/2$, the
classical delay margin for first-order feedback with pure delay. Define
controller $i$'s \emph{margin fraction}
\[
\rho_i \;=\; \frac{g_i \tau}{\pi/2} \;\in\; (0,1) .
\]
A controller tuned to consume half its delay margin has $\rho_i = 1/2$.

\begin{proposition}[Gain superposition]
\label{prop:superpose}
If the $m$ controllers act concurrently on the same plant variable, the
composed loop is
\[
\dot{x}(t) = -\Big(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m} g_i\Big)\, x(t - \tau) + d(t) ,
\]
which is asymptotically stable if and only if $\sum_{i=1}^{m} \rho_i < 1$:
marginally stable at equality and unstable above it. In particular,
any two controllers each tuned to consume half or more of the delay margin
compose to a loop that is not asymptotically stable, despite each being stable in isolation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}[Proof sketch]
Superposition of the corrections gives the stated composed loop, whose
stability condition is $\left(\sum_i g_i\right)\tau < \pi/2$. Dividing by
$\pi/2$ yields $\sum_i \rho_i < 1$.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{corollary}[Local tuning cannot prevent this]
\label{cor:local}
The margin fraction $\rho_i$ is determined by controller $i$'s own
configuration and the plant. The stability condition depends on
$\sum_j \rho_j$. No configuration surface enumerated in Section
\ref{sec:evidence} exposes $\rho_j$ for $j \neq i$, nor any proxy for it (Table \ref{tab:audit}).
The condition is therefore not evaluable, let alone enforceable, at any
individual controller.
\end{corollary}

Corollary \ref{cor:local} is the practical heart of the actuation-coupling
argument. The failure is not that operators tune badly. It is that the
quantity governing stability is not exposed anywhere an operator could read
it. Adding a second well-tuned controller to a well-tuned system can be
unsafe, and nothing in the audited tooling says so.

Unequal delays complicate the algebra without changing the conclusion. When
$\tau_i$ differ, the composed characteristic equation
$s + \sum_i g_i e^{-s\tau_i} = 0$ replaces the single-delay form. Numerically,
with $\rho_i$ defined on each controller's own delay, $\sum_i\rho_i<1$ remains
sufficient but becomes conservative: instability onset occurred at
$\sum\rho = 1.20$, $1.79$, and $2.00$ for equal-$\rho$ pairs with delay ratios
1:2, 1:10, and 1:100 (Section \ref{sec:results}). Widely separated delays,
such as shedder and autoscaler, are therefore the \emph{least} prone to this
channel; it bites hardest between mechanisms of similar speed.

\subsection{Observation coupling creates a threshold-ordering trap}

Now consider two controllers with different roles. A fast controller $F$
reduces offered load with output $u_F \geq 0$, and a slow controller $S$
provisions capacity from a measured demand signal. Crucially, $F$ acts
upstream of $S$'s measurement point, so $S$ observes
\[
\hat{\lambda} \;=\; \lambda - u_F
\]
rather than the true offered load $\lambda$. Let capacity be
$\mu = \mu_0 + u_S$ with $u_S = g_S(\hat{\lambda} - \mu)$, let
$u_F = g_F\, x$ with $g_F > 0$, and let congestion respond to capacity with
sensitivity $\kappa = -\partial x / \partial \mu > 0$.

\begin{proposition}[Signal masking]
\label{prop:masking}
The path $x \to u_F \to \hat{\lambda} \to u_S \to \mu \to x$ has positive sign.
Including the direct path by which $u_F$ itself relieves congestion, the
linearization $\dot x = -g_F x - \delta\mu$,
$\dot{\delta\mu} = g_S(-g_F x - \delta\mu)$ has eigenvalues $0$ and
$-(g_F+g_S)$: the masked operating point is neutrally stable, not unstable.
Capacity is not restored toward true demand; any shed level consistent with
$\delta\mu = \delta\lambda - g_F x$ is an equilibrium.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}[Proof sketch]
The system matrix $\begin{pmatrix}-g_F & -1\\ -g_Sg_F & -g_S\end{pmatrix}$ has
determinant $0$ and trace $-(g_F+g_S)$.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{corollary}[Threshold-ordering trap]
\label{cor:detune}
If the load reducer trips at a utilization $u_F^\ast$ below the provisioner's
target $u_S^\ast$, admitted load is held near $u_F^\ast\mu < u_S^\ast\mu$,
so the provisioner reads demand below its target and never scales up (and may
scale down). Changing $g_F$, $g_S$, or smoothing windows does not alter this
fixed point. It is escaped either structurally, by severing the dependence of
$\hat{\lambda}$ on $u_F$ (P2), or by retuning the thresholds so that
$u_S^\ast < u_F^\ast$.
\end{corollary}

In simulation (Section \ref{sec:results}) a shedder tripping at 60\%
utilization under a 70\% autoscaler target held the fleet at 6--8 replicas
where 18 were needed, shedding 64--70\% of load, across every combination of
shedder gain and stabilization window we tried; lowering the autoscaler target to 55\%
removed the trap entirely. The trap is thus a configuration-ordering hazard
that no single controller's surface flags, but it is not immune to retuning.

\subsection{Topological coupling has a branching threshold}

Let an endpoint be ejected and its load redistributed over the surviving
members of its pool. Let $\Gamma$ denote the expected number of additional
endpoints driven past the ejection threshold by the redistribution from one
ejection, given the current load margin distribution across the pool.

\begin{proposition}[Cascade subcriticality]
\label{prop:cascade}
Treating ejections as a branching process with mean offspring $\Gamma$, the
ejection cascade terminates almost surely if and only if $\Gamma < 1$. When
$\Gamma > 1$ the cascade reaches the pool's live-endpoint floor with positive
probability.
\end{proposition}

The practical content is that $\Gamma$ is computable before acting, from the
topology and the current per-endpoint headroom, which is what makes
topological coupling the most tractable of the three channels. Existing
guards (Envoy's \texttt{max\_ejection\_percent} and panic threshold
\cite{envoyoutlier,envoypanic}, Istio's \texttt{minHealthPercent}
\cite{istiodr}) bound ejection by fixed fractions rather than by $\Gamma$.

\section{Does the Premise Hold in Practice?}
\label{sec:evidence}

The model predicts trouble under two conditions: overlapping authority, and
independent configuration with no shared signal. This section tests
whether both conditions hold in widely deployed software. We use three
kinds of public evidence and deliberately avoid proprietary incident data,
so that every claim here is checkable by a reader.

\subsection{Configuration surface audit}

Method: for each mechanism, read the upstream documentation and
configuration reference, and record four fields. The signal the mechanism
reads. The plant variable it actuates. The tuning parameters it exposes.
Whether any documented hook allows it to observe or defer to a peer
mechanism. Table \ref{tab:audit} gives the schema and the mechanisms in
scope. We audit Kubernetes horizontal pod autoscaling and cluster
autoscaling, Envoy outlier detection, circuit breakers, adaptive concurrency,
local and global rate limiting, and retry budgets, Istio destination-rule
outlier detection, and Linkerd retry budgets.

\textbf{Findings.} (i) Several groups actuate one variable: three
mechanisms move the serving set, three bound admitted load, two add retries,
and the autoscaler and ejection move capacity in opposite directions. (ii) We
found \emph{no} documented hook linking autoscaling to any protection
mechanism. (iii) Contrary to our prior expectation of a uniformly empty
column, there are narrow intra-proxy hooks: Envoy's retry budget is a
circuit-breaker threshold \cite{envoycb}; Envoy re-admits ejected hosts in
panic mode and caps ejections \cite{envoyoutlier,envoypanic}; Istio disables
outlier detection when healthy hosts fall below \texttt{minHealthPercent}
\cite{istiodr}. (iv) Envoy's adaptive concurrency rejects with 503, a
``gateway failure'' that can feed consecutive-gateway-failure ejection
\cite{envoyoutlier}, an observation-coupling path inside one proxy. Our claim
is therefore narrowed to: no audited surface coordinates provisioning with
protection.

\subsection{Incident catalogue}

Public postmortems can show that these interactions occur outside
simulation. We use them for existence and mechanism, not for prevalence.
This is a deliberate methodological limit. Vendors publish selectively, and
most writeups lack the detail needed to diagnose a controller interaction,
so any rate computed over published incidents would be an artifact of
disclosure policy rather than a property of systems.

Method: rather than inherit a third-party curated list, whose maintenance we
cannot depend on, we index primary sources directly. We search vendor
incident archives from major cloud and infrastructure providers, apply stated
inclusion criteria, and code each admitted incident against the three
channels of Section \ref{sec:taxonomy}. Both authors will code independently and
report agreement. This catalogue is not yet compiled. The resulting index, with inclusion criteria and per
incident coding, is released as an artifact so that the catalogue does not
inherit our own maintenance either.

\subsection{Practitioner literature}

Independent of any dataset, the interactions we model are already named in
the canonical operational literature. The Google SRE treatment of cascading
failures describes overload propagation and the role of retries in
amplifying it \cite{sre2016}. The Amazon Builders' Library treats retry
amplification and load shedding as system-level concerns rather than local
ones \cite{buildersretry,buildersshed}. We cite this literature as evidence
that the phenomena are recognized, and position our contribution as supplying
the model and the remedy that it does not.

\section{Testing Method}
\label{sec:method}
We test agent composition in three layers, each pre-registered in a public
\texttt{PREREG.md} before the corresponding runs (v1--v3b). (i) A
\emph{static audit} (Section \ref{sec:evidence}) of what each agent senses and
actuates, to find pairs that share a variable or a signal. (ii) \emph{Model
checks} in a deterministic simulator, to see whether the structural conditions
of Section \ref{sec:model} behave as stated. (iii) \emph{Composition tests on
unmodified components}: two agents, each in a documented configuration, under
open-loop load, with a pass/fail threshold fixed in advance and a paired arm
that reverses one ordering. A trial is valid only if its load phase completes
and its metric sampling has no gap above 10\,s. Invalid trials are rerun and
reported.

\section{Model Checks in Simulation}
\label{sec:results}
Deterministic simulator; 20--30 seeds; 95\% normal-approximation CIs.
\textbf{Actuation coupling.} With $m=1..5$ controllers each at $\rho=0.6$ on a
common delay, independent composition diverges for every $m\ge2$, as
Proposition~\ref{prop:superpose} predicts. With unequal delays, instability
onset moved to $\sum\rho=1.20$, $1.79$ and $2.00$ for delay ratios 1:2, 1:10
and 1:100. \textbf{Observation coupling.} A shedder tripping at 60\% under
a 70\% autoscaler target held a fluid fleet at 6--8 replicas of 18 needed,
across all 12 combinations of gain and stabilization window. Lowering the
target to 55\% escaped. \textbf{Topological coupling.} In a 50-host pool,
ungated ejection collapsed the pool at mean utilization $\ge0.9$ and never
below; predicted $\Gamma\ge1$ matched realized collapse in 180/180 runs, but
outcomes were bimodal.

\section{Testing the Threshold-Ordering Trap}
\label{sec:testbed}

\textbf{Setup.} Single-node k3s v1.31.4 on one 8-vCPU VM. A Python service
has a capacity of about 50\,req/s per pod (4 workers, 80\,ms). Each pod has
an Envoy v1.31.4 sidecar shedder. An edge Envoy round-robins over pods. An
autoscaling/v2 HPA scales on per-pod 2xx rate (Prometheus with
prometheus-adapter), with a target of 35\,req/s (0.7 of capacity), 2--8

final 240\,s. Each experiment pairs a \emph{below-target} arm (shedding
engages below the HPA target) with an \emph{above-target} arm.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Token bucket} (PREREG v2): \texttt{local\_ratelimit} at $R=30$
vs.\ $40$\,req/s per pod.
\item \textbf{Adaptive concurrency} (v3b): \texttt{adaptive\_concurrency}

\item \textbf{Two tiers} (v3b): sidecar at 40\,req/s plus an edge

\end{itemize}

\section{Toward Arbitration}
\label{sec:design}
The model suggests an arbiter, \sys{}, that turns controllers into advisors.
It has one principle per channel. \textbf{P1}: one severity estimate and a
bounded authority budget allocated across actuators, so the summed margin
fraction is bounded by construction \cite{durham1993}. \textbf{P2}: serve each
controller a counterfactual input, e.g.\ admitted plus shed load for the
autoscaler. \textbf{P3}: admit a redistributive action only if projected
$\Gamma<1$. \textbf{P4}: route slow disturbance bands to slow actuators.
Each is implementable from outside the controllers. Apart from the
pre-registered P1 test in Section~\ref{sec:p1}, \emph{the evidence is
negative, inconclusive or marginal:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Simulation, full arbiter} (PREREG v1). The arbiter reached
attainment 0.916 [0.914, 0.917] against 0.993 for a fixed expert retuning
(lower HPA target, smaller gains). It did this at 0.69$\times$ the
retuning's replica cost, but failed RQ3. Retuning also escaped the trap,
failing RQ2. P1 alone did stabilize superposed gains (5.77\,s settling for every $m$).
\item \textbf{Testbed, P1 stand-in} (v3b). A static budget of edge 50 plus
sidecar 25\,req/s served 0.250 of load in 3/3 trials, \emph{worse} than
independent two-tier shedding (0.500). Its per-pod share (25) sat below the
HPA target (35), so it recreated the trap. It lowered rates rather than
allocating by severity, so it is not P1 as designed.
\item \textbf{Testbed, P3} (v3b). One failing endpoint among 10 produced
about 0.3\% errors and no cascade. All four ejection policies scored 0.997,
so the test could not discriminate.
\item \textbf{Testbed, threshold-aware P1, first run} (v4). An external controller
holds every sidecar trip point above 1.15$\times$ the HPA target and sizes the
edge budget to ready capacity, scaled by severity. It starts from the trap
configuration. \emph{Verdicts V1--V4 are inconclusive}: PREREG requires
three valid trials per arm, and only two P1 trials passed the
sampling-gap rule (0.698, 0.681). The third trial and its one allowed rerun
were invalidated by VM suspends (gaps of 1823\,s and 2405\,s). Descriptively, P1
beat the static trap (0.263, 0.263, 0.267; +0.42) but plateaued at 3 replicas
of 5, so it would likely have failed V2 (needs $\ge$5). The no-shedding arm
scored 1.000, 0.678 and 1.000. Its middle trial also stalled at 3 replicas.
In both stalls, the pods served about 36--37\,req/s each, within the HPA's
default 10\% tolerance of the 35\,req/s target. Under that reading, the
autoscaler saw no reason to scale: the plant, not P1, was miscalibrated.
Section~\ref{sec:p1} reports the corrected rerun. An INDEP trial contaminated by a leftover
controller process (0.694) was excluded and rerun cleanly. One aborted
P1 trial is retained and disclosed.
\item \textbf{Testbed, cascading pool with dynamic $\Gamma$} (v4). Ten pods of
mixed capacity at 0.9 load, one faulty. The cascade gate G0 passed: uncapped
ejection removed all 10 hosts in 2 of 3 trials. The $\Gamma$ controller
held peak ejections to 1--2 and ejected the faulty host in 3/3 trials (G3
PASS). It matched a static 10\% cap (G2 PASS; 0.915 vs.\ 0.919). It did not
reach the pre-registered goodput margin over uncapped ejection (G1 FAIL; 0.915
vs.\ 0.899, needed +0.05).
\end{itemize}
In short, a gate that prevents mass ejection is easy to build, but here it bought
little goodput. A budget must respect not only the shedder's threshold ordering but also
the autoscaler's tolerance band; once the testbed did, the threshold-aware
budget worked (Section~\ref{sec:p1}).

\subsection{A Confirmed Remedy: Threshold-Aware Budget (P1, v5)}
\label{sec:p1}
\textbf{Design.} PREREG v5 was committed before any v5 trial. It fixes the
v4 calibration fault: from v4 data, saturated pods served about 36.8\,req/s,
so the HPA target was lowered to 25\,req/s per pod (a saturated pod then
reads $\ge$1.47$\times$ target, well outside the 10\% band). Load steps from
50 to 160\,req/s for 480\,s. INDEP is the trap: sidecar concurrency cap 2
(trips near 18\,req/s per pod, below target). P1CTL starts from INDEP; the
controller raises each sidecar cap so no sidecar trips below
1.15$\times$ target, and sizes the edge budget to ready capacity scaled by
severity. SAFE (no effective shedding) is the reference. Five interleaved
trials per arm, wall-clock gap rule ($\le$10\,s), at most two reruns per
label. A gate W0 requires that SAFE actually scales ($\ge$4/5 trials at
$\ge$6 replicas). V1: P1CTL $\ge$ INDEP $+0.25$. V2: every P1CTL trial ends
with $\ge$5 replicas. V3: P1CTL $\ge$ SAFE $-0.05$. V4 (reported separately):
P1CTL p99 latency in the first 120\,s of the step $\le 0.8\times$ SAFE.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Control allocation and feedback computing.} Assigning corrective
effort among redundant actuators is well developed in aerospace
\cite{durham1993,johansen2013}, and feedback control of computing systems is
treated systematically by Hellerstein et al.\ \cite{hellerstein2004}. We borrow
the allocation view and add two channels the aerospace formulation lacks:
actuators that corrupt each other's sensors, and actions that redistribute
load across a graph.

\textbf{Autoscaling.} Autoscaling is studied as a control and prediction
problem \cite{lorido2014}; production systems such as Autopilot
\cite{autopilot2020} and FIRM \cite{firm2020} tune resources from observed
signals. These works assume the autoscaler's input reflects demand.
Corollary~\ref{cor:detune} and our testbed show when a co-located shedder
breaks that assumption.

\textbf{Overload and admission control.} Server overload control has a long
lineage \cite{welsh2003}. DAGOR coordinates load shedding across WeChat's
microservice graph \cite{dagor2018}, and Breakwater uses credits to admit
RPCs at microsecond scale \cite{breakwater2020}. Adaptive concurrency limits
import congestion control into the application layer \cite{netflixlimits}.
These systems coordinate \emph{one} mechanism across services. We study
uncoordinated \emph{different} mechanisms acting on one service.

\textbf{Metastable failures and retry storms.} Bronson et al.\ define
metastable failures, in which a trigger pushes a system into a bad state
sustained by its own feedback (often retries) \cite{bronson2021}. Huang et
al.\ find such failures across production incidents \cite{huang2022}. Retry
amplification is a canonical practitioner concern
\cite{sre2016,buildersretry}. Our threshold-ordering trap is related but
distinct. It is a stable equilibrium of two individually correct controllers
and needs no trigger beyond a load step. It persists because each agent's
own loop closes correctly.

\textbf{Cascading failure.} The branching threshold of
Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade} is standard in cascade models. Our use is as an
admission gate on an agent's action; Envoy's ejection cap and Istio's
\texttt{minHealthPercent} are fixed-fraction precursors
\cite{envoyoutlier,istiodr}.

\textbf{Testing agent interaction.} Agents validated alone can fail together.
Our contribution to this venue is a test method of three parts: a static
audit of what each agent senses and actuates, pre-registered composition tests
on real components, and an arbiter evaluated against an honest baseline.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{Construct validity.} Attainment (2xx over offered) and replica count
are proxies for user-facing objectives. Envoy's \texttt{local\_ratelimit} per
pod stands in for a capacity-relative shedder. The adaptive-concurrency
shedder was pinned to a fixed limit.

\textbf{Internal validity.} Thresholds were pre-registered
(\texttt{PREREG.md}) before each run. Trials with sampling gaps above 10\,s or
truncated load phases are discarded and rerun, and every such
trial is reported. The v5 P1 run had no invalid trials and used
wall-clock gap checks throughout. In the v4 campaign, samplers switched mid-campaign from
wall-clock to monotonic timestamps after VM suspends. Later trials therefore
cannot detect a suspend through the gap rule. Their load durations (from
vegeta's wall clock) were complete. The box hosting the testbed paused and was once rolled back to a
snapshot; the gap check exists for this reason. The $\Gamma$ controller
includes a disclosed exception that allows ejecting a host with under 10\%
success.

\textbf{External validity.} One node, synthetic services, and two or three
mechanisms at a time. Production fleets are larger, heterogeneous, and
configured differently. The simulator covers scale only in a model sense.

\textbf{Conclusion validity.} Testbed trials number two or three per arm
(five in the v5 P1 run). The v5 HPA target was chosen from v4 throughput
data; this was disclosed and pre-registered before any v5 trial, but the
confirmation rests on that calibration. The effects are large and consistent (below-target attainment $\le$0.50 vs.\ $\ge$0.99 above target), but small $n$ precludes fine-grained
estimates. Simulator CIs are normal approximations over 20--30 seeds.

\textbf{Negative results.} The full arbiter lost to expert retuning in
simulation (RQ2/RQ3). We report this rather than tune the baseline down.

\section{Artifact Availability}
The simulator, audit table with per-cell source URLs, testbed manifests,
trial scripts, raw replica traces, analysis code, and the pre-registrations
(\texttt{PREREG.md} v1--v5, committed before the corresponding runs) are public at
\url{https://github.com/pandeyaby/CONCERTO}. The testbed is reproducible on a
single VM at no cost (\texttt{testbed/setup.sh}).

\section{Conclusion}
Reliability agents that are each correct can fail together, and the
failures can be predicted, audited, and reproduced. The threshold-ordering
trap reproduced on unmodified Kubernetes and Envoy in every pre-registered
below-target trial, across three shedders and two application shapes, and
reversing the ordering escaped it every time. No audited configuration surface
would have warned the operator. Remedies are harder. A plain retuning beat our
simulated arbiter, and a naive shared budget recreated the trap it was meant
to prevent. A threshold-aware shared budget, once the testbed respected the
autoscaler's tolerance band, escaped the trap in its pre-registered test
(0.994 vs.\ 0.269, all trials at 6 replicas), though not with the latency
benefit we predicted, and on a single testbed with $n=5$. A $\Gamma$ gate
prevented mass ejection without a pre-registered goodput gain. We therefore
offer a tested account of failure, a method for finding such failures, and
one remedy that worked under test, with general arbitration as open work.
